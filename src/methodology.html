Eyewall

Methodology · data sources · known limits

Every storm condition in Eyewall is a reconstruction: NOAA's measurements of each storm's position, strength and size, run through published parametric wind and wave models. Nothing is a recording. Live storms use NOAA's preliminary operational data. Eyewall is not a forecast and must not be used for safety decisions: use the National Hurricane Center and your local National Weather Service office.

How the wind is reconstructed

For any place and time, Eyewall interpolates the storm's NOAA fixes (every 6 hours, plus landfall and other special points) and builds a wind field with the Holland, Belanger & Fritz (2010) profile. Its shape exponent is fitted separately in each quadrant so the profile passes through NOAA's 64, 50 and 34 kt wind radii, with the peak at the radius of maximum wind. Half the storm's forward speed is added for the asymmetry between its right and left sides, and the surface wind is turned 10–25° inward. Pressure follows the same profile from the central pressure to the outer pressure. Checked for Katrina at 18 UTC 28 Aug 2005: 150 kt at the radius of maximum wind, 902 mb centre, NOAA's radii matched within a few knots.

Gusts are shown as 1.2 × the sustained wind. That factor is a placeholder, not taken from a source.

The eye's radius comes from NOAA aircraft fixes when one is within a few hours (weighted by closeness in time), otherwise 0.62 × the radius of maximum wind.

How the waves are reconstructed

The highest significant wave height and peak period for each storm come from Young's (1988) equivalent-fetch model, checked against Young (2017, Atmosphere 8:194). Its fit covers maximum winds of 20–60 m/s; stronger storms are held at 60 m/s rather than extrapolated (so Katrina at peak gives 18.2 m). How waves spread out from that peak (falling off with distance, bigger on the right-front side) is a simple analytic shape of Eyewall's own, calibrated against buoys as described below. The moving sea surface is a JONSWAP spectrum scaled so the simulated surface has that wave height. Whitecaps level off and streaks take over as the wind rises, reaching a whiteout near 40 m/s (Holthuijsen, Powell & Pietrzak 2012).

For each case the buoy's hourly record from 36 hours before to 24 hours after the closest approach is compared, hour by hour, with what Eyewall's model gives at the buoy's position. What this says

  • Pressure is close: the Holland profile from NOAA's central pressure reproduces what buoys measured.
  • Wind is a little high overall (bias {{c.wind.bias}} m/s) and more so near the core ({{c.wind_near_core.bias}} m/s). Part of that is the buoys (anemometers a few metres up are sheltered by 10 m seas and buoys pitch), part may be the model.
  • Wave heights are right on average but individual peaks are not: the model gets the scale of the biggest seas right, not each one. Near the core it runs too high (bias {{c.all.hs_near_core.bias}} m); for the very biggest seas buoys recorded it is too low on average (bias {{c.all.hs_peak_big.bias}} m) and barely correlated. The largest, Katrina at buoy 42040, is in the table below. Storms becoming extratropical off New England (large, fast wind fields) are the worst cases.
  • Wave period is only loosely right.

Calibration

The analytic wave-spreading shape was fitted on storms from odd years and checked on storms from even years, which the fit never saw. Shrinking Young's peak gave the lowest hour-by-hour error but cut the biggest seas by about a third; forcing the biggest seas up made everything else worse on the held-out storms. So the peak, fall-off and asymmetry are left as they were, and the only change is an ambient sea of 1.5 m that is not made by the storm, added in quadrature. On the held-out storms that lowered the hour-by-hour wave-height error from {{b.held_out.hs.rmse}} m to {{c.held_out.hs.rmse}} m and the far-field bias from {{b.held_out.hs_far.bias}} m to {{c.held_out.hs_far.bias}} m, at the cost of a slightly larger near-core bias ({{b.held_out.hs_near_core.bias}} m to {{c.held_out.hs_near_core.bias}} m).

Sky, clouds, light

Sun position from standard solar formulas; the moon from the Astronomical Almanac's low-precision formulas, its brightness from Krisciunas & Schaefer (1991). Sky light follows the sun's elevation through twilight, with ozone colouring (after Hillaire 2020). Exposure adapts like a camera, and at night the image shifts toward how the eye sees in the dark. Clouds and rain are generated from the storm model (eyewall, eye, spiral bands). Within 12 hours of a bundled NASA photo, and only in daylight, the cloud layout from space comes from that photo. Lightning is decorative: there is no lightning data behind it.

Live storms

A scheduled job reads NHC's current-storms feed every three hours from 15 May to 30 November (monthly otherwise), with each storm's best track so far, its latest forecast and cone. The track so far is NOAA's preliminary operational best track and is revised after the season. The forecast and cone are drawn as NHC publishes them: the centre stays inside the cone about two thirds of the time, and impacts reach far beyond it. The dive runs only up to the latest fix; it never plays out the forecast.

Known limits

  • Every storm is a smooth parametric idealisation: no mesoscale vortices, eyewall replacement, land friction or shoaling, and no influence of the ocean floor on waves.
  • Radius of maximum wind before 2021 is operational, in 5 nm steps, and drives the size of the eye and eyewall.
  • Between NOAA's 6-hourly fixes everything is interpolated.
  • NASA photos exist only for major hurricanes, one per storm, from the daytime pass nearest its peak; pass times are estimated.
  • Deaths and damage are the reports' figures; 5 early Central Pacific storms are not compiled.
  • Gust factor is a placeholder (1.2 × sustained).
